🗺️ Mapping the Ecosystem

Think of the information world as an ecosystem — a vast, layered landscape of knowledge sources that exist in relationship to one another. Some are easy to reach; others are buried deep. Your job as a researcher or creator is to navigate this landscape with purpose.

🌱 Why not just Google it? Google shows you the surface — results ranked by popularity, SEO, and ads. The library gives you access to the deep layers: peer-reviewed research, licensed databases, rare archives, and expert guidance. Your tuition already pays for it.

The Layers of the Ecosystem

🌊 Surface Layer

Google, Wikipedia, news, social media. Easy to access, mixed quality. Good for background, not for evidence.

🏔️ Middle Layer

Library Search, Google Scholar, credible reports, gov data. Quality-filtered, accessible with the right tools.

⛏️ Deep Layer

Subject databases, special collections, archives, rare materials. The richest material — requires skill and access.

Today’s path: the terrain (source types) → pick the sources your assignment needs → navigation (Boolean logic & tools) → your pathway (finding & citing those sources at Tulane, including four pathways to data) → the cracks (inequities in what gets found) → AI in the ecosystem → guides, ILL, and expert help.

🧭 The Language of Search: Boolean Operators

Databases don’t understand questions — they understand logic. Boolean operators are the grammar of search. Master them, and you can navigate any layer of the ecosystem.

Step 1 — Break your question into concepts

Example question: “How does social media use affect depression among college students?”

Concepts: social media · depression · college students

Synonyms: social media → Instagram, TikTok · depression → anxiety, mental health · college students → undergraduates, young adults

Step 2 — Combine with Boolean operators

# AND — narrows (both terms must appear) social media AND depression AND "college students" # OR — broadens (use for synonyms) depression OR anxiety OR "mental health" # NOT — excludes a term "social media" NOT marketing

“Quotation marks”

Search an exact phrase: "college students". Without quotes, databases treat each word separately.

Truncation (*)

educat* finds: education, educator, educational. The asterisk is a wildcard for word endings.

# Putting it all together: ("social media" OR Instagram OR TikTok) AND (depress* OR anxi* OR "mental health") AND ("college students" OR undergrad*)

🔧 Troubleshooting Your Search

Searching is iterative — you’ll almost never get perfect results on the first try.

Too many results? ▼
Add more concepts with AND, use quotation marks, add date filters, or limit to peer-reviewed.
Too few results? ▼
Use OR for synonyms, try truncation (depress* instead of depression), remove one concept, or try a broader database.
Results aren’t relevant? ▼
Search “Subject” instead of “All Text”. Use NOT to exclude. Use subject headings from a good article.
Can’t access full text? ▼
Look for “FindIt@Tulane” links. Try Library Search. Use ILL — it’s free!
Not sure which database? ▼
Start with your subject’s Research Guide at libguides.tulane.edu. When in doubt, Academic Search Complete covers most disciplines.
Found a great article — how to find more? ▼
Check its references for earlier sources. Use “Cited by” in Google Scholar or Web of Science to find newer work. Copy its subject headings into your next search.
💡 Remember: A “failed” search isn’t wasted — it tells you what to adjust. Change one variable at a time.

🌱 Closing the Loop: Citing Your Sources

In a healthy ecosystem, nutrients cycle back into the soil. Citation works the same way: credit flows back to the scholars, communities, and creators whose work feeds yours — and your readers can retrace your path through the ecosystem.

Which style? Your assignment decides

APA, MLA, Chicago, CSE, and more — the style you use depends on your discipline and your instructor. Rather than memorizing formats, bookmark the library’s citation guide: it covers every major style with examples, links to the official manuals, and guidance on citing unusual sources (including AI-generated content).

⚖️ Cracks in the Ecosystem: Flaws & Inequities

The search ecosystem is powerful — but it’s not neutral. The systems that organize, publish, and surface knowledge carry historical biases that shape what you find and what you miss. Critical researchers and creators need to understand these cracks.

🚫 Who Gets Published?

The publishing pipeline has gatekeepers. Peer review favors established researchers at well-funded institutions. Scholars from the Global South, HBCUs, community colleges, and under-resourced institutions face systemic barriers to publication: higher rejection rates, less access to research funding, and fewer connections to editorial boards. If you only search peer-reviewed databases, you may be seeing a narrow slice of who actually produces knowledge.

🌐 Whose Knowledge Gets Indexed?

Language Bias

Major databases overwhelmingly index English-language journals. Research published in Spanish, Portuguese, Mandarin, Arabic, or Indigenous languages is often invisible to standard searches — even when it’s the most relevant work on a topic.

Subject Heading Bias

Library classification systems (like Library of Congress Subject Headings) were designed in the early 20th century and carry outdated or harmful language around race, gender, sexuality, disability, and Indigenous peoples. The terms you search with shape what you find.

💰 The Cost of Access

Academic publishing is a multi-billion-dollar industry. Most journal subscriptions cost thousands per year. Tulane’s library budget covers this for students — but researchers at less-resourced institutions, independent scholars, community organizations, and the general public are often locked out entirely. This paywall creates a knowledge divide.

💡 What You Can Do

Search beyond the usual databases. Look for open-access repositories (DOAJ, PubMed Central), institutional repositories, and grey literature (reports, working papers, theses).
Question your search terms. Try community-used language alongside academic jargon. Search in multiple languages if your topic warrants it.
Cite diversely and intentionally. Audit your bibliography. Are you citing women, scholars of color, Global South researchers, and non-Western perspectives? If not, ask why.
Use primary and community sources. Oral histories, archives, cultural collections, and community-generated knowledge are valid and often essential — especially for creative work.

🧬 A New Species in the Ecosystem: Weighing the Costs & Benefits of AI

As research and learning evolve in New Orleans and beyond, Tulane University Libraries’ Scholarly Engagement librarians are committed to up-to-date guidance on engaging with AI to find, evaluate, and cite information resources. The rise of AI — not only generative text models but also non-generative search assistants — has blurred the line between human and machine authorship while making it easier to skip the critical thinking that reading and writing require.

⚠️ Two costs that don’t show up on the screen. Outsourcing your thinking can leave real gaps in your research abilities — ultimately narrowing your prospects in fields that prize original thought. And training and running large generative models consumes substantial energy and water.

Gerlich, 2025; Hadi Mogavi et al., 2024; Yusuf et al., 2024 · Alnafrah, 2025; Bashir et al., 2024; Chouksey et al., 2026

The academic community is in the middle of an active, evolving conversation about AI outputs, plagiarism, copyright, and intellectual property (Cotton et al., 2024; Draxler et al., 2024; Ganjavi et al., 2024). To avoid academic integrity pitfalls — and to make sure you remain the true author of your work — apply the guidance below at each stage of your research.

What Is AI?

Artificial Intelligence is a broad term for computer systems designed to perform tasks that typically require human intelligence — recognizing patterns, making predictions, retrieving information, or producing new content — by learning from very large sets of data. “AI” is not one thing: it covers everything from the recommendation algorithm behind a streaming service to a chatbot that writes essays. Not all AI works the same way, and the distinction below matters for how, and whether, you might want to use a given tool at each stage of your research.

Generative vs. Non-Generative AI

⚡ Generative AI

Tools like ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude, and Microsoft Copilot create new text, images, summaries, or citations by predicting likely patterns from their training data. They do not consult a live database or verify facts by default — they produce plausible-sounding output, which is not the same as accurate output.

Because they train on general information, they’re a poor fit for scholarship sitting behind a paywall — though premium versions with broader access can partly mitigate this.

🔎 Non-Generative (AI-Assisted)

Tools like Semantic Scholar, Elicit, citation managers, and plagiarism checkers apply AI techniques to search, sort, filter, or organize existing information — without inventing new content.

They generally carry a lighter environmental footprint than generative tools.

The foundation for everything that follows: non-generative tools are generally “safer” to use throughout the research process, while generative tools carry real risks — to the accuracy of your work, and to your own development as a researcher. That said, every phase below has a genuine trade-off. Weigh the gains against the costs rather than reaching for a default.

🌳 Cost-Benefit Analysis by Research Phase

💡

1 · Brainstorming a Topic

What do I want to research?

⚡ Generative AI
Gains
  • Topic suggestions in seconds.
Costs
  • Verifying suggested topics still takes time later.
  • Topics are less original, constrained by model training.
  • No access to paywalled resources.
🔎 Non-Generative AI
Gains
  • Quick recommendations to relevant literature.
  • Surfaces scholarly relationships when mapping tools are included.
Costs

  • Vendors may log your searches.
📚

2 · Finding Books, Articles & Other Resources

What sources do I need for my research?

⚡ Generative AI
Gains
  • Resources are suggested quickly.
  • May surface search terms you missed.
Costs
  • Fabricated citations mean verifying every single result.
  • Misses discipline-relevant databases.
  • Paywalled scholarship is invisible.
🔎 Non-Generative AI
Gains
  • Semantic search and cluster graphs speed discovery.
  • Surfaces adjacent conversations that keyword search misses.
  • May connect directly to library databases.
Costs
  • Rankings favor citation count over novelty — newer or niche work gets overlooked.
⚖️

3 · Evaluating What You’ve Found

How do I know what I found is relevant and credible?

⚡ Generative AI
Gains
  • Summaries in seconds.
  • Summaries can be tailored to help you navigate unfamiliar jargon.
Costs
  • Summarization is computationally demanding, carrying heavy environmental costs.
  • You may have to read the full text anyway to understand the output.
  • Doesn’t build the mental models and critical thinking that reading builds.
  • Summaries mirror the bias in your prompts.
  • Uploading licensed content may violate terms and retain your data.
🔎 Non-Generative AI
Gains
  • Quick access to reference and citation metrics.
  • Citation mapping shows a source’s place in the wider conversation.
Costs
  • Metrics used to score relevance may miss some literature.
🔗

4 · Generating Citations

How do I keep track of what I find and use?

⚡ Generative AI
Gains
  • Quick outputs.
Costs
  • Every generated citation needs manual verification.
  • Paywalled DOIs and page numbers are hard for AI to locate.
🔎 Non-Generative AI
Gains
  • Automates formatting.
  • Structured metadata generation by design.
Costs
  • Some manual checks are still needed for formatting details.
🔒

5 · Feeding Library-Owned & Licensed Resources into AI

Can I upload the PDFs I got through the library?

⚡ Generative AI
Gains
  • Instant summary, analysis, or data extraction across lengthy PDFs.
  • High-level comparative synthesis across multiple documents at once.
Costs
  • Significant energy and compute footprint for multi-page document digestion.
  • Uploading may violate university policies or publisher terms — risking campus-wide access termination.
  • Reliance on AI summaries weakens synthesis capacity and critical evaluation.
  • Consumer AI tools often retain uploaded content to train future models.
🔎 Non-Generative AI
Gains
  • Fast indexing, metadata extraction, and semantic search within personal document managers.
  • Keeps PDFs organized without exposing their content to third parties.
Costs
  • Automated extraction can miss nuanced arguments.
  • Requires learning local tools (Zotero, PDF readers) rather than relying on server storage.
  • Some copyright restrictions still apply.

📌 TUL Librarian recommendation: use the AI tools embedded in vendor databases — they operate within the library’s licensing terms, so you sidestep the copyright and access-termination risks that come with uploading licensed PDFs into consumer tools.

🤖 AI & Academic Research at Tulane

Now that we’ve defined what AI is and where it belongs in the research process, here’s how Tulane expects you to use it. The university takes a pro-exploration approach — but with clear expectations around transparency, integrity, and data privacy.

Tulane’s Core Stance

Tulane does not ban AI — and actively encourages students, faculty, and staff to explore its potential. However, the university’s Academic Integrity Policy applies fully to AI use. Any AI assistance beyond grammar and spell-checking on graded work must be explicitly permitted by your instructor. Faculty are now required to include an AI statement in their syllabus — check it every semester.

Key Guidelines for Students

🔍 Transparency First

Policies vary by course and instructor. If AI use is permitted, describe how you used it. Check your syllabus AI statement before every assignment.

🔒 Protect University Data

Do not enter Tulane data classified as Internal, Confidential, or Restricted into publicly available AI tools. Information shared with AI using default settings is not private.

⚠️ Verify Everything

AI tools can produce inaccurate, biased, or fabricated results — including fake citations. Always verify AI-generated content against real sources before using it in academic work.

🏛️ Tulane AI Resources

  • ai.tulane.edu — Tulane’s official AI hub
  • Library AI Research Guide

If You Do Use Generative AI, Always Consider…

Even where a tool is permitted, weigh these before you rely on it for brainstorming or finding resources:

  1. Citations may be completely fabricated.
  2. The tool may miss important details in a resource.
  3. You can stifle your creative and critical thinking by outsourcing those skills to the algorithm.
  4. AI tools — especially generative ones — can use your information without your knowledge to train future models.
  5. Using AI-generated text can be considered plagiarism and grounds for academic discipline.
  6. You should always disclose your use of AI.
  7. The environmental impacts of AI may not be fully known for some time.
  8. Legal regulations on AI could lead to more limited use in the future.
  9. AI can’t read resources behind paywalls, limiting the sources it can recommend.
