Why Use the Library for Research?

You have access to Google—so why bother with the library? Great question!

🔍 Google Gives You...

  • Billions of results (too many!)
  • Results ranked by popularity, not quality
  • Ads and sponsored content
  • Paywalled articles you can't access
  • Mixed reliability—anyone can publish

📚 The Library Gives You...

  • Curated, high-quality sources
  • Expert-reviewed scholarship
  • Free access to expensive databases
  • Full-text articles and books
  • Librarians who can help you
💡 Fun Fact: Your tuition gives you access to millions of dollars worth of research databases, journals, and books. Not using the library is like paying for an all-you-can-eat buffet and only eating the breadsticks!

From Topic to Search: Terms & Boolean Operators

Finding good sources starts with good search terms, then combining them strategically with Boolean operators.

Step 1: Identify Key Concepts

Break your topic into its main ideas. Pull out the important nouns and concepts.

Example: "How does social media use affect depression rates among college students?"

Key Concepts: Social media | Depression | College students

Step 2: Brainstorm Synonyms

Key ConceptSynonyms & Related Terms
Social mediaFacebook, Instagram, TikTok, social networking, online platforms
DepressionMental health, anxiety, psychological well-being, mood disorders
College studentsUniversity students, undergraduates, young adults, emerging adults

Step 3: Combine with Boolean Operators

# AND — Narrows (both terms must appear) social media AND depression AND college students → Results must include ALL three concepts # OR — Broadens (either term can appear) depression OR anxiety OR mental health → Results can include ANY of these terms # NOT — Excludes terms social media NOT marketing → Removes results about social media marketing

More Techniques

"Quotation Marks"

Search exact phrases: "social media addiction", "college students"

Without quotes, databases search each word separately.

Truncation (*)

depress* → depression, depressed, depressive

teen* → teen, teens, teenager, teenagers

The asterisk acts as a wildcard.

Putting It Together

# A sophisticated search combining techniques: ("social media" OR Instagram OR TikTok) AND (depress* OR anxi* OR "mental health") AND ("college students" OR undergrad*)
💡 Pro Tip: Look at keywords in articles you've already found to discover more search terms!

What Is a Database?

Definition: A database is a structured, searchable collection of information—typically journal articles, book chapters, reports, or other scholarly works—organized so you can find sources on specific topics. Think of it as a specialized search engine for academic content, with filters for date, subject, peer-review status, and more.

Unlike a general web search, databases only contain vetted, cataloged sources and give you powerful tools to narrow your results precisely.

When to Use Which Tool

🔍 Start with Library Search when:

  • You're new to a topic and exploring
  • You need both books and articles
  • You want one search box for everything
  • You're not sure which database to use yet

🔬 Switch to a subject database when:

  • You need comprehensive results in one field
  • Library Search gives too many irrelevant results
  • You want to use subject headings or a thesaurus
  • Your professor recommends a specific database
💡 Pro Tip: Library Search is great for casting a wide net, but subject databases give you a finer mesh. Start broad with Library Search, then go deep with a subject database for your best results!

Library Databases: Your Research Powerhouse

Databases are searchable collections of articles, books, and other sources. Unlike Google, they're organized, high-quality, and you have full access through the library!

Types of Databases

🔬 Subject-Specific Databases

Focus on one discipline. Best for in-depth research in your major.

Examples: PsycINFO (psychology), PubMed (health/medicine), JSTOR (humanities), IEEE Xplore (engineering)

🌐 Multidisciplinary Databases

Cover many subjects. Great for interdisciplinary topics or getting started.

Examples: Academic Search Complete, Web of Science, Library Search at Tulane, ProQuest Central

📰 News & Current Events

Newspapers, magazines, and news archives.

Examples: Nexis Uni, Newspaper Source Plus, Nola.com (Times-Picayune), Access World News

🎬 Streaming Media & Primary Sources

Films, music, archival documents, and historical collections.

Examples: Films on Demand, Digital Theatre+, Archives Unbound, Black Studies Center

Demo: Setting Up Google Scholar with Tulane

Google Scholar is powerful for academic literature. Connect it to Tulane for free full-text access.

Without Tulane Links:

  • Titles and abstracts only
  • Paywalls ($30-50 per article!)
  • Miss library subscriptions

With Tulane Links:

  • One-click full text access
  • Free through library subscriptions
  • "Full Text @ Tulane" links appear

How to Set It Up (One-Time)

  1. Go to scholar.google.com
  2. Click the menu (☰) in the top left
  3. Select Settings
  4. Click Library links
  5. Search "Tulane" and check Tulane University - Full Text @ Tulane
  6. Click Save
✅ You're all set! Now you'll see "Full Text @ Tulane" links in Google Scholar results.

🔧 Troubleshooting Your Search Results

Too many results? ▼
Add more concepts with AND, use quotation marks for exact phrases, add date filters, or limit to peer-reviewed sources.
Too few results? ▼
Use OR to add synonyms, try truncation (depress* instead of depression), remove one concept, or try a broader database like Academic Search Complete.
Results aren't relevant? ▼
Try searching "Subject" instead of "All Text". Use NOT to exclude irrelevant topics. Look at subject headings of a good article and use those terms.
Can't access full text? ▼
Look for "Full Text @ Tulane" or "FindIt@Tulane" links. Try searching the title in Library Search. As a last resort, use Interlibrary Loan—it's free!
